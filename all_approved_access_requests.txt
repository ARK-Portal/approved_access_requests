Rheumatoid Arthritis (RA) is an autoimmune inflammatory joint disease that affects adult females more than males, with 70% of patients being female. While women suffer from more autoimmune disease than men, they also experience fewer pathogen infections and improved vaccine response. Despite observation of this phenomenon for over 30 years, mechanistic understanding is limited and there are no sex-specific autoimmune therapies that capitalize on sex differences of the immune system.
In the past year, we have utilized the histology from AMP Phase 2 to build a computational pathology model for cell typing. This work is represented in this manuscript (Bell et al, 2024, Nat Commun., PMCID: PMC11362542). In the next year, we plan to use the histology and scRNAseq data to improve the H&E cell typing modeling.
In the past year, we have used the scRNAseq data to investigate sex difference within each identified synovial cell cluster using cell frequency based tools, like Mixed-effects modeling of Associations of Single Cells (MASC), and differential gene expression analysis. We are also comparing and contrasting the myeloid subsets with a mouse inflammatory arthritis model to demonstrate relevance. These analyses use computational and machine learning approaches to measure the similarities between the datasets. Preliminary results were presented at the American College of Rheumatology and are currently being incorporated into a manuscript.
In the past year, we have a manuscript under review at eLIFE demonstrating the synergy of PGE and TNF to stimulate similar pathways to RA. The reviewers requested a formal similarity analysis to a fiducial RA synovial NGS dataset, and we decided to use AMP Phase 2 to assess. These analysis and results are being prepared currently to incorporate into a manuscript review submission to this manuscript (https://elifesciences.org/reviewed-preprints/104367 ).

Project Lead: Paula Ramos

Data Access Request Submitted By: @PaulaSRamos
Intended Data Use Statement (accepted on 4/18/2025):
The goal of this project is to understand the role of impaired X-chromosome inactivation (XCI) in autoimmune disease. It specifically seeks to systematically identify cell-specific X-linked genes that escape XCI in patients with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) and rheumatoid arthritis (RA). It will leverage AMP RA/SLE genotypic and single cell gene expression data on over 120 patients with SLE, 70 with RA, and 20 controls identify the spectrum of X-linked genes that escape XCI across specific cell subsets. Specifically, it will use allelic-specific expression (ASE) to distinguish the single cell and allele-specific expression of the two X-chromosomal haplotypes. As the inference of allele-specific phenomena in single cells is complicated by widespread monoallelic expression, it will leverage genotype phase information to detect sites where the expressed allele is discordant with the active X chromosome. We expect to confirm XCI escapees previously identified in autoimmune conditions and, given our larger sample size, to identify more genes in more cell subsets that escape XCI.

What do you want to do?
MontageBio has developed a computational platform for identifying somatic mutations that drive or modify disease.  We detect these from bulk RNA samples of affected tissue.  We are analyzing existing samples for a range of immune and inflammatory diseases, including Rheumatoid Arthritis and Lupus.  The pipeline identifies genes undergoing somatic remodeling by looking at mutation patterns across many samples from affected tissues, using a combination of traditional RNA analysis and advanced machine-learning approaches to tease out rare mutations.  Why are you doing it?
Our ultimate goal is to identify genes that are either disease-driving or protective, with the long-term goal of developing or repurposing medication to treat that disease via insights gained from somatically mutated genes.

How do you want to do it?
Our platform is cloud-based (AWS), secure, and private, with all samples and downstream analysis segregated by sample and data collection. Data are accessible only to authorized personnel within Montage.  We ultimately generate aggregated gene-level analysis.  Computation typically takes a few days for a collection, from raw FastQ sequence data to ranked genes.


Project Lead: Prof Maria Bokarewa
Institution: Department of Rheumatology and Inflammation Research, Institution of Medicine, University of Gothenburg, Sweden
Data Access Request Submitted By: @venkataragavan
Intended Data Use Statement (accepted on 2/11/2025):
Survivin is an oncoprotein, and in healthy tissue, it is essential for cell renewal and differentiation. Canonical cellular functions of survivin include inhibition of apoptosis and regulation of cell cycle through formation of the chromosomal passenger complex. Survivin is intricately involved in the pathogenesis of autoimmune diseases. For example, RA patients with joint erosion exhibit high plasma and serum survivin levels which correlate with disease activity. Since structural studies show survivin's interaction with Threonine-3 of the histone H3 tail, the goal of this project is to explore survivin's role in epigenetic regulation through interaction with histone tail post-translational modifications, specifically lysine-trimethylation and lysine acetylation.

We performed chromatin-immunoprecipitation (ChIP) experiments targeting survivin in CD4 cells sorted from PBMCs of healthy and RA patients. In our recently published study (link - https://doi.org/10.1186/s12964-024-01814-4) we demonstrated that survivin interacts strongly with clustered histone tail modifications. The survivin-histone tail interaction is sequence-specific and enriched for transcription factor binding motifs such as chromatin remodelling complexes and histone tail modifiers. Survivin inhibition redistributes the studied modifications, specifically increasing the level of methylation-specific lysine modifications and decreasing levels of lysine acetylation. By recruiting the BRG1 complex, survivin modulates DNA damage response in blood CD4+ cells. Using the dataset of scRNAseq from synovial biopsies of RA patients, we showed that the markers of the cytotoxic T cell clusters were also upregulated in BRG1-hi blood CD4+ cells.

Building from this study, we now aim to investigate the metabolic profile of the CD4+ T cell clusters found in synovia and pinpoint the pathogenic metabolically active clusters. Further, we aim to study the effect of insulin on the metabolic activity of the different T cell clusters. By combining this data with our inhouse ChIPseq data targeting histone modifications and survivin, we plan to characterize the insulin-dependent effect on the genome binding of survivin and histone modifications, and further study its phenotypic effect on the different T cell clusters found in RA.

OBJECTIVES OF THE PROPOSED RESEARCH
The grand objective of this project is to generate a T-cell receptor (TCR) atlas by collecting TCR data of patients with different chronic autoimmune arthritis diseases, as well as simultaneously generating bulk and single-cell TCR-seq data from our own arthritis patient cohort. This atlas will significantly aid the (i) understanding of pathophysiology and discovery of biomarkers; (ii) (sub-category) diagnosis; (iii) prediction of the course of disease post-therapy and recommendation of appropriate treatments.

STUDY DESIGN AND ANALYSIS PLAN
Identification of Arthritis Associated TCRs
We will generate bulk and single-cell TCR-seq data from our own cohort of patients suffering from different kinds of autoimmune arthritis Their TCR repertoires will be characterised on two levels: globally and locally. Globally, we will assess the TCR clonality, diversity and clonotype overlap across the different T-cell subsets and different groups of arthritis patients. Locally, we aim to drill down into the repertoire and identify groups of similar TCRs that are uniquely shared across a specific patient group, implying a mechanistic role. To overcome repertoire sparsity, we will employ clustering approaches, which group similar TCRs into epitope/MHC-restricted clusters, hence increasing our statistical power in finding shared TCR patterns. After clustering, we will perform a statistical enrichment analysis to extract these TCR groups that are significantly associated with a specific arthritis subgroup and absent in control (healthy) repertoires. Informative TCR clusters will be prioritized based on their generation probability (how likely they are to form), extent of expansion, and convergence (indicating an epitope-driven selective pressure).

Annotation of TCR specificity
Further annotation (e.g., for viral epitope reactive TCRs) will be performed by matching TCRs against those in curated databases of TCRs with known specificity. Furthermore, if exact matches are not available in such databases, we can rely on more complex machine learning algorithms to perform specificity annotations. Concretely, with this task, we aim to derive a defined set of TCR patterns that can be thought to closely linked to chronic autoimmune arthritis. To validate and assess the robustness of these TCR signatures, we will test their presence in extensive, independent patient cohorts. Our approach involves compiling a database of synovial fluid TCRs from publicly available sources. This is where we would want to use ARK data for. This database will represent unique TCR clonotypes in a wide range of autoimmune arthritis. By utilizing this diverse and comprehensive dataset, we aim to rigorously evaluate the generalizability and consistency of the identified TCR patterns across different forms of arthritis. This method not only underscores the validity of our findings but also offers insights into the potential clinical applicability of these TCR signatures.

As platelets are anucleate, these data indicate that MK, the cells that produce platelets and afford them their RNA content, are altered in SLE. We will integrate these intriguing findings by examining MKs in SLE with emphasis on MK bioenergetics.
HYPOTHESIS: An auto-immune prone environment reprograms MKs into a pro-inflammatory phenotype

Moreover, we will proceed with comparisons with available platelet transcriptome databases, and will determine similarities or divergences between MK and platelet transcriptomes in these diseases. Integrate tissue-derived immune cell and PBMC (and other biofluid) single-cell/nucleus RNA-seq data into a common framework using a standardized pipeline to integrate disparate datasets.
After integration, identify the full ensemble of gene expression programs in each major cell class/type in the unified data set.
Using the integrated dataset, evaluate the role of individual immune expression programs in each cell type across available datasets (for tissue and other biosamples) to identify which cell types program fulfil roles in different inflammatory diseases.
Integrate AMP data with other available datasets (from De Jager and external sources).
Integrate multiple sclerosis PBMC, CSF and brain single-cell/nucleus RNA-seq data into the AMP integrative analysis as a bridge between the neurodegenerative diseases and inflammatory diseases (from De Jager and external sources).
Identify molecular programs in the non-immune cells from the target tissue that correlate to the immune programs in #3.
Validate associations using emerging single-cell/nucleus RNA-seq data as well as bulk data (i.e., RNA, proteomics, and metabolomics).
